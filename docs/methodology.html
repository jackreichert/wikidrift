Methodology

How WikiDrift decides what to surface, and the discipline that keeps it a lead to investigate rather than an accusation. Everything here runs on public data and is reproducible.

A lead for a researcher, not a verdict.

The problem, and why it needs two tools

Billions of people meet a topic through a single Wikipedia article, shaped by a relatively small number of editors. It was begun in good faith, an extraordinary volunteer effort. But it is also human, self-governed, and subject to bad actors, its disputes settled by consensus among whoever shows up, and an article can end up skewed in two very different ways that cannot be found the same way:

  • Capture by change, where an article was rewritten over time to shift its story. WikiDrift is built to catch this, by reading an article against its own past.
  • Capture by origin, where an article carried a point of view from the very start, or a like-minded majority kept it that way. There is no earlier, cleaner version to compare against, so reading the article's own history tells you nothing. This needs an external reference, and it is the hard limit we describe below.

The founding choice: judge the text, not the editors

The obvious approach, taken by some prior efforts, is to start from a list of suspect editors and flag whatever they touch. We deliberately do not. Editors who work the same contested topic naturally cluster, edit near each other, and vote alike, whether or not anything coordinated is going on, so "they cluster" proves nothing on its own. Building on such a list bakes in that circular reasoning and makes the tool just another partisan actor.

Instead, WikiDrift flags an article only by its own trajectory. A named list, if used at all, is an optional, sourced, swappable overlay laid on top, never the foundation. The mechanism works identically whatever a list contains, and that neutrality of mechanism is the point.

What we measure: the layers

The engine is built in layers, each answering a narrower question than the last. The lower layers work on any article from public data alone; the upper layers add language and meaning.

LayerQuestion it answers How
L1 — Drift & pivotsWhen was the article most heavily rewritten? Weighs each word by how long it survived (persistence-weighted loss), finds the moments long-stable text collapsed (pivots), then binary-searches the exact revision.
AttributionWho removed the old text, and who wrote the new? Names the editors of the removed and added words, from public revision data. Action, not intent.
L2 — FramingDid the meaning shift, not just the words? Rates stance on a neutrality scale (not sentiment) over time, so a reframing shows up whether it was done by adding slanted text or removing critical text.
L5 #1 — Cross-lingualDo other language editions frame it differently? Runs the same framing read across editions (natively, no translation), both as they stand now and before vs. after a pivot.
L5 #2 — FactsDo the editions actually disagree on the facts? Compares answers to load-bearing factual questions across editions, with an as-of date, to catch numerical or categorical contradictions.
L5 #3b — SourcesWhat kinds of sources does it cite, and how did that change? Reads the article's own citations and shows what the sourcing changed from → to across a pivot — which source-domains were added or dropped, and the shift in citation types. It rates no source; it shows the change and lets you judge.

A further signal, a controversy measure (how much an article was edit-warred), is used only as context. On its own it cannot separate a genuinely contested topic from a captured one, so a low controversy score on an otherwise flagged article is itself informative: the change was made quietly, without a fight.

The single most important idea

Text that survived years of readers and editors has earned a stability prior. A passage stable for a decade that is then reversed and kept against attempts to restore it is hard to explain innocently. But no single signal is proof. A finding is only strong on the conjunction: long-stable, reversed, previously-sourced text removed, persisting despite reverts, by a narrow set of editors. Any one factor alone is noise.

What keeps it honest

These are not polish. Each one is a lesson from something that broke during development.

  • Change is not bias. The largest single rewrite across our whole test slate was a completely benign restructuring of the Climate change article, bigger than anything on Zionism. So the raw change signal is a change detector, not a bias detector. That is precisely why the framing and cross-lingual layers exist: to tell a legitimate rewrite from a capture.
  • Prove it on a control first. Every metric is checked against a neutral topic (Photosynthesis) before any positive result is believed. Early versions mistook a vandalism blip, and a control's normal early growth, for "pivots"; the fixes (snapshotting on stable revisions, requiring the change to actually stick) came from catching those false alarms.
  • Do not suppress an honest signal. When the method surfaced a real but trivial old rewrite on a clean article, we did not re-tune it to hide it. A change detector should report change; the judgment of whether it matters happens in the layers above.
  • Age never demotes a finding. A distortion that stood for fifteen years is not "stale", it is a prime target. We rank findings by how much stable content was displaced, not by how recent they are.

The known limit, and the answer

Because WikiDrift reads an article against its own history, it is blind by design to bias that was there from the start: an article born with a point of view, a distortion held in place by a like-minded majority, or one that simply stood unchallenged for years. To that kind of tool, those all look perfectly healthy. There is also a direction ambiguity: it can see that stable text was removed and the change stuck, but not whether that change injected bias or corrected it.

Resolving either requires looking outside the article, which is what the cross-lingual and fact layers begin to do, and what a plurality of independent encyclopedias would do fully. The aim is never to declare a single neutral truth. It is to make disagreement legible: show where independent authorities diverge, with the receipts, and let the reader judge.

Why you can check it

Every finding traces to specific public Wikipedia revisions, listed as receipts on each article page. The tool uses only public data (token authorship from WikiWho, the Wikipedia API, and Wikidata to match articles across languages; a language model reads framing and factual claims). It is open-source, and the change-detection layer has been checked against a set of independently adjudicated cases: it caught 5 of 6 known content-removal cases, and the local engine reproduced the reference results on all 8.

What's new here

Nearly every part of the machinery below already existed. WikiDrift's contribution is the combination, and a way of framing the problem, not a new algorithm. As far as we can tell, no prior tool or paper puts these together:

  • It finds the moment and the hands. Earlier work either detects that an article changed (from activity spikes, or a date a human tagged it) or traces who wrote a given word. WikiDrift discovers when an article's long-stable core was dismantled from the text itself, with no tag and no activity proxy, and then names who removed the old words and who wrote the new ones at that exact pivot. Joining unsupervised discovery to attribution is the gap nobody else occupies.
  • Two kinds of capture, told apart. We separate bias introduced by change from bias present from the start, and we say plainly that a tool reading an article against its own past cannot see a distortion that was always there. Naming that blind spot, and building the external-reference layer as its answer, is part of the design rather than a caveat buried at the end.
  • Divergence anchored to the moment of change. Comparing how language editions portray a topic is an old idea. Asking whether the English edition peeled away from the others at the very pivot the engine found, instead of merely noting that they differ today, turns a static comparison into a test that can tell a capture from a legitimate real-world event.

And a quieter difference. In a field crowded with efforts that name suspect editors and present the result as proof, the disciplined refusal to do that is the real distinction: judge the text, not the editors; report a lead, never a verdict; and demonstrate, on a neutral control, that change is not bias. The honesty is the feature. The shoulders it stands on are below.

Sources & prior work

WikiDrift does not reinvent the science. It composes established, peer-reviewed work, and its originality is the combination. These are the shoulders it stands on, and the findings that keep it honest.

Token authorship — the engine

  • Fabian Flöck & Maribel Acosta, WikiWho: Precise and Efficient Attribution of Authorship of Revisioned Content (WWW 2014), and TokTrack (ICWSM 2017) — per-token provenance across an article's whole history.
  • The open-source wikiwho_rs engine, to which this project contributed a dump-parser fix.

Measuring a rewrite — content survival

  • B. Thomas Adler & Luca de Alfaro, A Content-Driven Reputation System for Wikipedia (WWW 2007, WikiTrust).
  • Aaron Halfaker et al., persistent-word-revisions (WikiSym 2009) — the basis for our persistence-weighted measure of how much durable text a rewrite displaced.

Framing over time, and the caution we took to heart

  • Umashanthi Pavalanathan, Xiaochuang Han & Jacob Eisenstein, Mind Your POV (CSCW 2018) — article framing measured over time.
  • Isaac Johnson, Recommended Practices for NPOV Research on Wikipedia (2025) — why we rate stance on a neutrality axis rather than sentiment, which conflates tone with viewpoint balance.

Controversy signal

  • Taha Yasseri et al., the mutual-revert controversy measure (PLOS ONE 2012) — our contextual controversy corroborator.

Cross-lingual comparison — the external-reference layer

  • Patti Bao, Brent Hecht et al., Omnipedia (CHI 2012), and Manypedia (Massa & Scrinzi) — comparing how different language editions portray the same topic, the idea behind our cross-lingual layer.

Findings that keep us honest

  • Shane Greenstein & Feng Zhu (AER 2012; MIS Quarterly 2018) found an article's slant rarely shifts through revision — a direct caution against reading every rewrite as capture.
  • Independent work comparing Wikipedia with Britannica found it no more biased on the topics studied — a counter-finding we build into the framing rather than ignore.
  • Jan Grabowski & Shira Klein, on documented distortion of Holocaust history on Wikipedia (2023) — the peer-reviewed case behind the "Warsaw concentration camp" example here, showing a long-standing distortion can stand unchallenged for years. Exactly what the external reference layer targets.

What we deliberately did not build on

Several advocacy efforts, across the political spectrum, take a "name the suspect editors" approach: start from a list and treat who-edits-with-whom as proof of coordination. We reject that as circular and unfair, and built the opposite, judging the text, not the editors. Such reports are contested and are, at most, motivating context, never an input to a finding.

What it will not do

It will not accuse an individual editor of bad faith, it will not declare a neutral truth, and it will not call any article "biased". It points you somewhere to look, and shows its work.
